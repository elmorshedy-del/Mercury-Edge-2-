# One analog port rations every ASOS call

Every call to a public ASOS number ends on **one analog voice port** at the station, and that port explains all three things a Twilio caller sees. The FAA's telecom plan gives each ASOS three dial-up circuits. Two carry maintenance and data, and **one is "dedicated to provide computer-generated voice weather messages to public users,"** with the public telephone interface listed as **"No. Channels: 1."** The legacy station computer speaks the observation through dedicated voice boards and a voice modem. Its ASOS 2.0 replacement, the Campbell AeroX Audio105, still has a single RJ11 line pair. On the network side, the FAA sponsors the line. After a 2023–2026 renumbering, 68 of 82 sampled major-airport ASOS numbers now sit in Bandwidth or Onvoy VoIP-type number blocks, so most calls probably reach the station through an IP gateway feeding that same single port (strong inference). **Seizure has no queue.** The first call to reach the switch or gateway after the port goes idle takes it. The line is busy to everyone from the moment it starts ringing, and nothing remembers who waited. **Busy** is that switch or gateway reporting the port occupied: SIP 486 or ISUP cause 17, which Twilio records as `busy`. For a single line, Erlang's formula puts busy at **60% of attempts at just one attempt per minute**. It also caps the line at **about 38–40 connected calls an hour**, and one persistent automated caller can keep it occupied about 90% of the time. The **~90-second hang-up** is the station's own sharing mechanism, not a Twilio or carrier limit. The FAA's written standard for non-federal automated weather phones (AWOS) is to join the caller to the loop already playing and "automatically disconnect when the weather observation has been completely transmitted twice." Two plays of a 55–70-word message at ASOS's documented 100 words per minute come to roughly 70–110 s. No public ASOS document states the station's own ring count, repeat count or timer. The two-play rule for ASOS is therefore a strong inference, and ordinary Twilio call records can test it.

## Five links separate a Twilio call from the spoken observation

From end to end, a Twilio call to an ASOS number is an ordinary outbound phone call whose far end is a weather computer. There are five elements in the chain. Twilio places the call and reports whatever the far end signals. The terminating carrier routes it. An access element rings the station's single voice port or rejects the call as busy: a central-office switch on copper, or a gateway on an IP-delivered line. Inside the station's Acquisition Control Unit (ACU), the voice port answers, and the voice software plays its looping observation and hangs up. The table separates what each element decides and how firmly that is known.

| Link | What it is | What it decides | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Twilio Programmable Voice | Originating platform | Account-level call rate and concurrency. It relabels the far end's signalling as `busy`, `no-answer`, `failed` or `completed` | Documented ([Twilio Call resource](https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/api/call-resource)) |
| 2. Terminating carrier | Number-block holder: Bandwidth.com or Onvoy at 68 of 82 sampled airports; AT&T incumbent blocks at KMDW and KAUS | Routing; analytics-based blocking, if any, signalled as SIP 603+ | Block holders documented; current carrier after any porting not checked |
| 3. Access element | Incumbent switch plus copper loop, or an analog telephone adapter, hosted platform or POTS-replacement box | **Busy or ring** for each new call; release and re-idle timing | Strong inference from number blocks and the FAA/NWS copper-to-IP goal |
| 4. ASOS voice port | Legacy: voice modem in the ACU modem rack. ASOS 2.0: Audio105 RJ11, one Tip/Ring pair | Answer (off-hook) and hang-up (on-hook) | One public voice circuit and one port documented |
| 5. ACU voice software | Builds speech from a fixed vocabulary at 100 wpm; same audio as the ground-to-air radio | Content (one-minute observation or last METAR); repeat count and disconnect | Content documented; repeat and disconnect rule inferred |

The three questions (who gets the line, why it is busy, why it hangs up) map onto this chain. Seizure is decided at link 3, by arrival order alone. Busy is link 3 reporting that link 4 is already off-hook or ringing. The ~90 s hang-up comes from link 5. The first two follow from standard single-line telephony and are high confidence. The third rests on an FAA standard written for a sister system and on arithmetic, because the ASOS-specific parameters sit in manuals the NWS does not publish.

## Inside the station, one circuit of three serves the public

The legacy ACU is still in service at all seven target stations. It generates speech on cards in its VME rack, a **Voice Processor Board (1A2A20)** and a **Voice Recorder/Playback Board (1A2A21)**. It reaches telephone lines through a separate rack of individual telephone modems. A continuous self-test checks those modems every 7 minutes "as long as the modems are not busy" ([S100 ASOS Site Technical Manual](https://www.scribd.com/document/74787732/KXP)). The FAA's April 2000 telecommunications plan gives the clearest statement of line count: "Each ASOS has three dial-up commercial circuits. Two are used for remote maintenance monitoring functions and one is dedicated to provide computer-generated voice weather messages to public users." Its interface table lists the ASOS-to-public-network link as **"No. Channels: 1"** on a local dial-up modem ([FAA Future Telecommunications Plan, 2000](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/58105/dot_58105_DS1.pdf)). AWOS, by contrast, uses "a single dial-up circuit" for both voice and maintenance (same source). The 2017 ASOS modernization paper describes the same building blocks, a "modem AC power rack and telephone modems" and voice boards that ASOS 2.0 replaces with "the audio card in the computer and the new audio distribution amplifier" ([Hays et al., AMS 2017](https://ams.confex.com/ams/97Annual/webprogram/Manuscript/Paper315698/AMS%20Modernization%20of%20ASOS%20Hardware%20and%20Software%20Paper_FINAL.pdf)). No public source names the voice-modem model or says which card detects ringing and takes the line off-hook. The likely signal chain, a **medium-confidence reconstruction**, runs from the CPU that builds the message, to the voice processor that strings recorded vocabulary words together, to audio that feeds both the radio and the voice telephone interface. Nothing in the ASOS record matches the AWOS design norm that an answerer "typically" handles five calls at once ([FAA AC 150/5220-16E](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_150_5220-16E.pdf)).

The software side is better documented. Voice messages are "spoken at the rate of 100 words per-minute" and are identical on the telephone and the ground-to-air radio. Each message "begins with an identification of the location, the phrase 'AUTOMATED WEATHER OBSERVATION' and the UTC (ZULU) time," then gives each element with a spoken prefix, then the word "remarks" and any automated or manually entered remarks ([ASOS User's Guide §6.5](https://www.weather.gov/media/asos/aum-toc.pdf)). The line carries **"Either OMO or last transmitted METAR/SPECI – Not both"** ([ASOS data-outlet chart](https://www.weather.gov/media/asos/aum-charts2.pdf)). That choice is the only documented operator setting for the voice output. A controller selects it on the station's Operator Interface Device under CMD → VOICE → TYPE. Unstaffed sites "must always be set to broadcast the OMO data," and "ASOS defaults to the One-Minute-Observation after a system reboot." In last-METAR mode, a correction (COR) makes "the telephone broadcast … unavailable until the next METAR/SPECI is issued" ([FAA JO 7900.5E](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Order/JO_7900.5E_with_Change_1.pdf)). Site configuration files include "VOICE AIRPORT NAME" and "CMD VOICE/PASSW" ([ASOS v2.6A release note](https://www.weather.gov/media/asos/ASOS%20Implementation/relnoteprocup.pdf)). The one-minute observation (OMO) is computed early in each minute. Processing starts at :00 and "ends promptly at 23 seconds after" ([IEM ASOS note](https://mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/ASOS/precipnote.phtml)). The phone keeps carrying the one-minute value even during the hourly METAR lockout ([Twin & Turbine](https://twinandturbine.com/issue/June11/files/basic-html/page22.html)).

**Maintenance and archive traffic does not use the public number.** The User's Guide separates the public dial-in from the "ASOS remote user dial-in port." That port is reserved for authorized users with an access code and password ([ASOS User's Guide](https://www.weather.gov/media/asos/aum-toc.pdf)). The NWS AUTODIAL backup, which polls a site after a missed METAR, uses "a separate line" ([NTSB docket CEN19MA141](https://data.ntsb.gov/Docket/Document/docBLOB?FileExtension=pdf&FileName=WX_CEN19MA141AB_Attachment_1_redacted-Rel.pdf&ID=8134624)). NCEI collects one-minute data through its own modem bank, dialing about 960 stations on an 11-hour cycle ([NCEI ATRAC record](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/archive/atrac/export/2022-01-13T14-11-44.pdf?id=65639)). On legacy ASOS, therefore, maintenance sessions and NCEI collection should never make the voice number busy (inference, medium-high confidence). The radio is a parallel audio output and does not occupy the phone line either.

ASOS 2.0 changes the hardware but not the line count. Campbell Scientific lists the **AeroX Audio105** among the products used in the US ASOS replacement ([Campbell case study](https://s.campbellsci.com/documents/us/case-studies/united-states-asos-weather-network.pdf)). Its telephone interface is **a single RJ11 "VOICE MODEM" socket whose pinout lists only "Ring line 1" and "Tip line 1,"** wired to an internal single-board computer and supporting "voice and DTFM [sic] decoding as required by NOAA." Its Ethernet port exists only so an ASOS server can "control and generate the audio messages," and a second Audio105, where fitted, merely stands by to take over the same audio stream ([AeroX Audio105 manual](https://s.campbellsci.com/documents/us/manuals/aerox-audio105.pdf)). The manual does not say what the touch-tone decoding is for, and it is not a documented public function. None of the seven stations had moved to ASOS 2.0 as of the NWS equipment sheet dated 18 March 2025, whose in-service tab is empty ([NWS ASOS equipment](https://www.weather.gov/asos/asosequip.html)). In April 2026 Campbell said the agencies were still "conducting their final acceptance testing" ([Campbell](https://www.campbellsci.com/resources/case-studies/us-asos-weather-network)). KMDW and KAUS are FAA-owned and exposed to the FAA's transition of 207 ASOS sites to AWOS-C over 2025–2029 ([NWS ASOS FAQ](https://www.weather.gov/asos/FAQ.html)), which could change their voice equipment and numbers. The inference that swapping the station computer leaves capacity at one caller per number is medium-high confidence.

## Most big-airport ASOS numbers left copper between 2023 and 2026

The FAA, not the NWS, is responsible for the line. The User's Guide calls the service "FAA sponsored telephone dial-in access" ([ASOS User's Guide](https://www.weather.gov/media/asos/aum-toc.pdf)). The 2004 communications transfer plan says "The FAA will order all telco circuits. The NWS will connect to the ASOS ACU" ([ASOS Communications Transfer Implementation Plan](https://www.weather.gov/media/asos/ASOS%20Implementation/ACTIP031604new.pdf)). In a 2019 NTSB docket, the NWS wrote that the FAA Technical Operations office in Anchorage "has the responsibility for PAKT ASOS telephone circuits," with AT&T as "the primary telephone company for this circuit" ([NTSB CEN19MA141](https://data.ntsb.gov/Docket/Document/docBLOB?FileExtension=pdf&FileName=WX_CEN19MA141AB_Attachment_1_redacted-Rel.pdf&ID=8134624)). The FAA's telecom contracts are moving off copper. FENS, awarded to Verizon in 2023 for about $2.4 billion over 15 years, succeeds the older FTI contract. It is the FAA's way to "shift away from … time division multiplexing … that typically goes through copper wires" ([Washington Technology](https://www.washingtontechnology.com/companies/2023/03/faa-selects-verizon-24b-telecommunications-contract/384517/); [Verizon](https://www.verizon.com/about/news/verizon-public-sector-wins-federal-aviation-administration-fens-contract)). The ASOS program's own goal is to "Replace all copper voice and data lines with Internet Protocol communications" by end of 2026 ([Boutin, FPAW 2023](https://fpaw.aero/sites/default/files/163/2-boutin-decadal-look-asos-lifecycle-upgrades.pdf)).

The phone numbers show the migration has largely happened at big airports. On 10 October 2026, 82 major-airport ASOS numbers were sampled from AirNav, which republishes FAA Chart Supplement data. Each number's original block holder was looked up in LocalCallingGuide's prefix data, which is derived from the national number-assignment database ([AirNav KDEN](https://www.airnav.com/airport/KDEN); [LocalCallingGuide](https://www.localcallingguide.com/xmlprefix.php?npa=719&nxx=204&blocks=1)). Every site lists exactly one ASOS number. **68 of the 82 sit in number blocks held by wholesale VoIP-type carriers: Bandwidth.com (58) and Onvoy (10).** Thirteen sit in incumbent Bell blocks and one in MCImetro. Many of the VoIP-type numbers belong to rate centers far from the airport. KDEN's 719 number, for example, is a Cañon City number. That pattern is typical of hosted voice service, not a copper loop from the airport's local exchange. Wayback captures cover 11 airports' number history, and every one swapped an incumbent number for a VoIP-type carrier number between about 2023 and 2026. KDEN moved from a Qwest 303 number (June 2024) to the Bandwidth 719 number by August 2025. KLAX moved from a Pacific Bell number to a Bandwidth number by the same month ([Wayback KDEN 2024](https://web.archive.org/web/20240604201354/https://airnav.com/airport/kden); [Wayback KDEN 2025](https://web.archive.org/web/20250813172701/https://www.airnav.com/airport/KDEN); [Wayback KLAX 2023](https://web.archive.org/web/20230611055842/https://www.airnav.com/airport/KLAX); [Wayback KLAX 2025](https://web.archive.org/web/20250816090636/http://www.airnav.com/airport/KLAX)). No FAA statement explains the change.

**Strong inference** (high confidence that the numbers were re-provisioned, medium that delivery is IP): most of these lines now arrive over IP at an adapter or POTS-replacement box that presents one analog line to the same ASOS voice port. A block holder is the carrier originally assigned the block, and a number can later be ported to another carrier. Only a number-level portability lookup would confirm today's carrier.

IP delivery moves the busy decision from a central-office switch to whatever device owns the analog port. That device's configuration changes what a second caller experiences (inference, medium confidence). With call waiting off, the second caller gets SIP 486 and Twilio shows `busy`, exactly as on copper. With call waiting on, the second caller instead hears ringing that the ASOS, which cannot switch between calls, never answers, and Twilio ends the attempt as `no-answer` at its ring timeout; the busy signals Mercury's calls receive suggest call waiting is off on the lines being called. If the gateway or its wireless backhaul is down, callers get "temporarily unavailable" or timeout responses rather than busy. KMDW and KAUS are still in AT&T incumbent blocks. AT&T plans to shut down most of its copper by end of 2029, and FCC-approved discontinuance in about 500 wire centers took effect in June 2026 ([Light Reading 2024](https://www.lightreading.com/regulatory-politics/how-and-when-at-t-will-kiss-copper-goodbye); [Light Reading 2025](https://www.lightreading.com/broadband/at-t-s-copper-retirement-plan-plows-ahead); [AT&T](https://about.att.com/blogs/2026/upgrade-home-phone-and-internet.html)). AT&T's replacement home-phone product lists call waiting as a feature ([Light Reading 2024](https://www.lightreading.com/regulatory-politics/how-and-when-at-t-will-kiss-copper-goodbye)), so a carrier-driven conversion could change how those two lines behave. Migration also retires numbers. A stored pre-2023 number such as KDEN's old 303 number returns a not-in-service failure, not busy, or reaches an unrelated subscriber.

## The first call to arrive after release seizes the line

A single analog line has no queue and no camp-on, and it has no call waiting unless deliberately enabled. Each new call is judged on its own at the moment it reaches the access element. If the port is idle, the element assigns the call to it and starts ringing. **From that instant the line is busy to everyone else, before the ASOS has even answered.** If the port is off-hook, ringing, or still in its short post-release guard interval, the element rejects the call. On a traditional circuit-switched path, the terminating switch releases it with cause 17, "user busy." On an IP path, the gateway answers SIP 486 Busy Here, which RFC 3398 defines as the equivalent of cause 17 ([RFC 3398](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3398.html); [ISUP release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Release_(ISUP))). The busy tone itself means "the called line has been reached but it is engaged in another call" ([Cisco call progress tones](https://docstore.mik.ua/univercd/cc/td/doc/product/tel_pswt/vco_prod/sup_call/sup04.htm)). Nothing records failed attempts. A caller who has been redialing for ten minutes has no priority over a first-time caller, and the caller who just finished can get the line again.

Queueing theory calls this a retrial system. Blocked callers wait in an "orbit," and under the classical model "the retrial rate is proportional to the number of customers in the orbit" because each "cannot observe the behavior of the others" ([Tuan, retrial-queue survey](http://infoshako.sk.tsukuba.ac.jp/~tuan/papers/Tuan_chapter_ver3.pdf); [Falin & Templeton, *Retrial Queues*](https://www.routledge.com/Retrial-Queues/Falin-Templeton/p/book/9780412785504)). With independent callers, each caller's chance of taking the next idle period is proportional to how often it attempts. That is the whole allocation rule. There is no fairness mechanism, no priority for pilots, and no memory of who waited. It also explains why the design shares the line through a forced hang-up rather than a queue. Teletraffic engineering describes forced disconnection as the standard way to make it "impossible for a single call to block vital parts of the exchange" ([Iversen, *Teletraffic Engineering*, 2015](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/118473571/Teletraffic_34342_V_B_Iversen_2015.pdf)). It follows that the line's turnovers go mostly to whoever attempts most often. That is an externality on low-persistence callers, not a service any caller is entitled to.

Between one call ending and the next caller hearing audio sits a short, mostly fixed chain of steps, timed here from generic switch documentation rather than ASOS sources (medium confidence on the numbers). The ASOS goes on-hook after its second play. The switch or gateway declares a disconnect only after the line has been open longer than its hook-flash window, which one Cisco analog port sets at "Loop open for > 800 ms" ([Cisco supervision signaling](https://docstore.mik.ua/univercd/cc/td/doc/product/tel_pswt/vco_prod/sys_adm/swsaa.htm)). The release is then signalled to the network, and the port sits in a guard interval during which it "cannot be allocated to another call," 800 ms in Cisco's example ([Cisco call supervision](https://docstore.mik.ua/univercd/cc/td/doc/product/tel_pswt/vco_prod/sup_call/sup03.pdf)). On an IP-delivered line, the port is therefore typically assignable again about a second or two after the ASOS hangs up. On a copper line with timed release it can take several seconds. The next call then rings the station. Suppose ASOS follows the AWOS practice of answering "prior to completion of the second ring" ([AC 150/5220-16E](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_150_5220-16E.pdf)) at the standard 2 s on / 4 s off ring cadence ([Precise Tone Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precise_Tone_Plan)). Audio would then start within roughly 2–8 s. Each connected call therefore holds the port for about 95–100 s in total.

One unknown matters for anyone trying to use the line lightly. When the caller hangs up first, the ASOS learns of it only if two things hold. First, the line must deliver a disconnect signal. Plain loop-start service "imposes no requirement to the central office end to inform the subscriber end of distant far-end disconnection conditions" ([Loop start](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loop_start)). Second, the ASOS voice board must act on that signal. Neither is documented for ASOS. **If either is missing, the station keeps the port off-hook until its own two-play cycle ends. The line then stays busy for the full ~90 s however early the caller hangs up** (inference, low-medium confidence). Hanging up promptly remains the courteous default, but it cannot be assumed to free the line sooner.

## One line saturates at about 40 connected calls an hour

A single line with no queue is Erlang's loss system with one server. The probability that an attempt hears busy is **B = A/(1+A)**, where A is the offered load: attempts per unit time multiplied by the holding time ([ITU-T E.800 Supplement 1](https://www.itu.int/rec/dologin_pub.asp?id=T-REC-E.800SerSup1-198811-I%21%21PDF-E&lang=s&type=items); [Iversen 2015](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/118473571/Teletraffic_34342_V_B_Iversen_2015.pdf)). Erlang's formula is "valid for arbitrary holding time distributions," so a near-constant 90 s call is covered exactly ([Iversen 2015, §4.6.2](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/118473571/Teletraffic_34342_V_B_Iversen_2015.pdf)). With random arrivals, the busy probability also equals the fraction of time the line is occupied. The table is calculated for this report with a 90 s holding time. The attempt rates are illustrative, because no ASOS call data is public.

| Attempts per minute, all callers combined | Offered load (Erlang) | Share of attempts that hear busy | Connected calls per hour |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 (6 per hour) | 0.15 | 13% | 5.2 |
| 0.5 | 0.75 | 43% | 17.1 |
| **1** | 1.5 | **60%** | 24.0 |
| 5 | 7.5 | 88% | 35.3 |
| 20 | 30 | 97% | 38.7 |
| Upper limit | — | → 100% | → 40 |

Two features of the curve explain what Mercury's calls see. Busy climbs steeply even at light load: **one attempt per minute from everyone combined already makes 60% of attempts busy.** And capacity has a hard ceiling of 3,600 ÷ 90 = **40 connected calls an hour**, or about 38 once ringing is counted. Beyond roughly one attempt a minute, extra attempts almost all hear busy and add almost no completed calls.

Erlang's formula assumes blocked callers go away. Real callers redial. Kosten's warning applies: "any theoretical result that does not take into consideration this repetition effect should be considered suspect" ([Artalejo & Gómez-Corral, *Retrial Queueing Systems*](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-540-78725-9)). Classic telephone measurements found that a subscriber who hits busy repeats the call within an hour 70% of the time, and becomes more persistent after each failure ([Iversen 2015, §1.10](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/118473571/Teletraffic_34342_V_B_Iversen_2015.pdf)). In the single-server retrial model, line occupancy equals the underlying demand however fast blocked callers redial, and faster redialing only multiplies the attempts that hear busy. A calculation for this report shows the size of the effect, using the standard model with 90 s calls. With demand of 20 wanted calls an hour, the line is idle half the time. Yet if blocked callers retry every 5 s, about **95% of all attempts hear busy**, and each completed call costs about 20 attempts. **A caller's own busy rate therefore overstates congestion.** On its own, it cannot reveal how many other parties are calling.

Four kinds of caller compete for the line. **Pilots** have reported busy ASOS lines for years, "usually when a crazy front is coming through and there are a bunch of us all trying to see how strong the winds are at that exact second" ([Pilots of America, 2016](https://www.pilotsofamerica.com/community/threads/phone-number-for-atis.98234/)), and a 2020 pilot guide warns that "only one call can go through at a time!" ([TxTop Aviation](https://txtopaviation.com/how-to-get-the-current-weather/)). **Relay services**, such as the patented anyAWOS bridge, dial the same lines with built-in busy-and-retry handling ([US Patent 7,088,241](https://www.freepatentsonline.com/7088241.html); [anyAWOS FAQ](https://www.anyawos.com/SubscFAQ.html)). **Prediction-market traders and their bots** are the newest group. Wethr.net describes an "OMO Bot" that automates calls to the dial-in system, notes that "often, only one call can access the ASOS line at a time," and warns of "cities where OMO bots are known to be active" ([Wethr.net market bots](https://wethr.net/edu/market-bots)). An open-source Polymarket dashboard ships a Twilio/Telnyx calling agent scoped to the 20–35 minutes before a METAR near a market bucket edge, capped at "a handful of short calls per day per city" because "these lines exist for pilots" ([testedmedia/polymarket-weather-command-center](https://github.com/testedmedia/polymarket-weather-command-center)). Finally, **KNYC is not an airport**, so essentially all of its callers are non-pilots.

Demand from traders is growing and spread through the day. Kalshi's weather vertical is "expected to bring in $1.1 billion in trading volume this year" ([The Independent](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/americas/kalshi-weather-channel-betting-partnership-b3040492.html)). Its hourly markets settle on an index built from one-minute ASOS data ([CFTC filing](https://www.cftc.gov/filings/ptc/ptc09092624308.pdf)), which spreads trader interest across all daylight hours rather than only around the daily high. Pilot phone use at large airline airports such as KLAX and KDEN is probably small (inference, medium confidence). No FAA or NWS rule addresses automated callers on these lines.

A discrete-event simulation built for this report shows how little automated demand it takes to dominate the line. In the model, pilots arrive at random at 6 calls an hour, retry about a minute after a busy, and give up after five tries; automated callers call again a fixed interval after each call or busy; and each call holds the line for 90 s plus 4 s of ringing.

| Persistent automated callers (re-call interval) | Line occupancy | Pilots who connect within five tries | Pilot attempts that hear busy |
|---|---|---|---|
| None | 16% | 98.5% | 26% |
| One (600 s) | 27% | 97.4% | 40% |
| One (60 s) | 66% | 85.3% | 68% |
| One (10 s) | 91% | 35.5% | 92% |
| Three (3 s, 10 s, 60 s) | 98% | 11.2% | 98% |

The exact numbers matter less than the shape. **One persistent automated caller per station is enough to keep the line busy about 90% of the time.** Near-constant busy signals at market-sensitive moments therefore need only a handful of automated callers. The cost falls on pilots who call once or twice and give up, and that is the outcome the two-play disconnect exists to limit.

Contention is the main cause of busy signals, but other failures exist, and each leaves a different signature. In ordinary telephony "lines may become seized and be unavailable for use without indicators" ([Avaya](https://support.avaya.com/public/index?id=SOLN104584&page=content)); **a fault that leaves the ASOS port off-hook gives busy around the clock, including at 3 a.m.** That belongs in a trouble report to the AOMC, the NWS ASOS operations and monitoring center (aomc@noaa.gov, 800-242-8194) ([NWS ASOS FAQ](https://www.weather.gov/asos/FAQ.html)), or through the FAA's new public dashboard for reporting AWOS/ASOS outages ([AVweb](https://avweb.com/aviation-news/faa-wants-pilots-to-flag-bad-awos-data/)); FAA status data already logs ASOS outages under "Non-FAA Lines, Circuits, or Leased/Provisioned Services" ([FAA WeatherCams stations](https://weathercams.faa.gov/stations)). A dead voice board on a working line rings without answer and shows as `no-answer`, not busy. A tower-set last-METAR outlet after a correction yields no message rather than busy. The ACU's modem self-test is at most a speculative contributor to brief busies, because the manual says busy modems are skipped. And some failures never reach the station at all: carrier congestion, Twilio account limits and analytics-based call blocking each show distinctly in the call record, as the final section sets out.

## The station, not Twilio, hangs up after two plays

The written rule exists only for non-federal AWOS. FAA Advisory Circular 150/5220-16E says the telephone answerer should answer "prior to completion of the second ring," put "the audio signal in progress at the time the call is received" on the line, and **"automatically disconnect when the weather observation has been completely transmitted twice."** Messages loop "with approximately a 5-second delay," and the periodic maintenance check confirms the phone "answers on second ring … provides two complete messages and disconnects" ([AC 150/5220-16E](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_150_5220-16E.pdf)). State procurement specifications copy the disconnect rule verbatim ([Illinois DOT AWOS specification](https://apps.dot.illinois.gov/eplan/desenv/042823/GR012-16A/GR012-16A.pdf)).

**No public ASOS document states the station's rings-to-answer, repeat count or disconnect timer.** That covers the User's Guide, JO 7900.5E, the v2.6A and v3.10 release notes, the NWS maintenance instruction and the AMS papers. The documents that likely would state them are not public: Section IV of the S100 technical manual, the ASOS Software User's Manual, the ASOS 2.0 software specifications, and Engineering Handbook 11, which the NWS says is "not available to the general public" ([NWS ASOS FAQ](https://www.weather.gov/asos/FAQ.html)). The site database does contain telephony timers such as "BUSY ATTEMPT TIME" and "SEND REPLY TIME(SECS): 120." These govern ASOS's own data communications, not the voice line ([ASOS v2.6A release note](https://www.weather.gov/media/asos/ASOS%20Implementation/relnoteprocup.pdf)).

**Strong inference (medium-high confidence, roughly 70% for a two-play count rule versus a fixed timer):** ASOS answers within a ring or two, joins the caller to the loop already playing, and hangs up after two complete transmissions. Three things support this. The FAA treats AWOS and ASOS as a single component from the telecommunications perspective ([FAA Future Telecommunications Plan §7-02.2.1](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/58105/dot_58105_DS1.pdf)). The phone and the radio share one continuous loop. And the arithmetic fits. At the documented 100 words per minute ([ASOS User's Guide](https://www.weather.gov/media/asos/aum-toc.pdf)), with a 5 s gap between plays, calculated holding times are:

| Words in message | One play | Two plays plus gap | Typical total if caller joins mid-message | Maximum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 45 | 27 s | 59 s | ~75 s | ~91 s |
| 55 | 33 s | 71 s | ~90 s | ~109 s |
| 65 | 39 s | 83 s | ~105 s | ~127 s |
| 75 | 45 s | 95 s | ~120 s | ~145 s |

A temperature-relevant one-minute message runs about 50–70 words. It covers station name, "automated weather observation," Zulu time, wind, visibility, sky, temperature, dew point, altimeter and remarks. That predicts roughly 70–110 s, centred on the observed ~90 s. Joining mid-loop explains call-to-call variation of 15–20 s. The FAA's description of an AWOS message as "a 20 to 30 second weather message updated each minute" ([FAA AIM 7-1-10](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap7_section_1.html)) fits the shorter end. These word counts are estimates, because no recorded ASOS message length was found.

Neither Twilio nor the carrier imposes the 90 s limit. Twilio's `Timeout` governs ringing before answer: 60 s by default, 600 s at most, and 30 s for `<Dial>`. It has nothing to do with how long a connected call lasts ([Twilio Call resource](https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/api/call-resource)). When the station hangs up, Voice Insights records `disconnected_by` as the callee, after a normal answer, with a `connect_duration` near 90 s ([Twilio Voice Insights](https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/voice-insights/api/call/call-summary-resource)). The disconnect is the line's sharing mechanism, so it should persist through ASOS 2.0 and the IP migration. New call-handling software could change the details (low-medium confidence).

| Behaviour | Set by | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| One public voice line, one caller at a time | Site telecom design and the single voice port | High |
| Busy to a second caller | Access switch or gateway, with call waiting off | High |
| One-minute observation vs last METAR | Tower via operator terminal CMD → VOICE → TYPE; unstaffed sites must voice OMO; reboot defaults to OMO | High (documented) |
| Airport name, manual voice remarks | Site database and operator input | High that they exist |
| Speech rate of 100 wpm | Fixed in software | High (documented) |
| Rings before answer | Probably fixed in station software | Low-medium |
| Repeat count and disconnect | Probably fixed in station software; no documented setting | Medium |
| Gap between loops | Unknown for ASOS (AWOS about 5 s) | Low |

The two-play rule also limits what one connected call can tell you. The AWOS standard says a message already playing "should be completed without interruption," with the new observation starting after the next gap ([AC 150/5220-16E](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_150_5220-16E.pdf)). If ASOS works the same way, a value computed by :23 past the minute enters the loop only at the next message boundary. A ~90 s call then spans one or two distinct minute values, and a spoken value can be up to one message length old (inference, medium confidence). In last-METAR mode, the spoken Zulu time never advances.

## Twilio's call records separate normal busy signals from faults

Twilio says it "can only reflect the signaling it receives from the carrier," and recommends checking "the CallStatus and 'Last SIP Response' for each call" ([Twilio Support](https://support.twilio.com/hc/en-us/articles/53558413407003-Detecting-When-a-Recipient-Is-Already-on-Another-Call-With-Twilio-Programmable-Voice)). Classifying each outcome is the most useful diagnostic, and it needs no change in calling behaviour. The mapping below combines Twilio's documented statuses, RFC 3398's mapping between telephone-network cause codes and SIP responses, the North American tone plan, and the FCC's call-blocking signalling rule. Twilio publishes no complete table from SIP response to call status, so rows marked inferred should be confirmed against the account's own Voice Insights fields: `last_sip_response_num`, `pdd_ms`, `disconnected_by` and `connect_duration`.

| What the call record shows | Meaning | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| `busy`, last SIP 486 or 600, Debugger 31486, short post-dial delay | The station's port is off-hook or already ringing for another caller: normal contention | Documented ([Twilio Support](https://support.twilio.com/hc/en-us/articles/53558413407003-Detecting-When-a-Recipient-Is-Already-on-Another-Call-With-Twilio-Programmable-Voice)) |
| `completed`, `disconnected_by` callee, `connect_duration` about 70–110 s | Normal call ended by the station's two-play disconnect | Inferred |
| `no-answer` after the full ring timeout, with ringing events | The line rings but nothing answers: dead voice board, misrouting, or call waiting enabled on an IP port | Inferred ([Twilio error 34003](https://www.twilio.com/docs/api/errors/34003)) |
| `no-answer` or `failed` with SIP 408 or 480 | No response or temporarily unavailable; on IP lines, gateway offline | Inferred from [RFC 3398](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3398.html) |
| `failed` with SIP 503 | Network congestion upstream, heard as fast busy; not the station | Inferred from [RFC 3398](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3398.html) |
| SIP 603 with a `Reason` header containing `v=analytics1` (603+) | Analytics-based blocking by a provider in the path, and a signal to stop and reassess call volume. The header is documented for Elastic SIP Trunking packet captures; how Programmable Voice labels such calls is undocumented | [47 CFR 64.1200(k)(9)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-64/subpart-L/section-64.1200); [Twilio changelog](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/changelog/elastic-sip-trunking---pass-on-603--reason-header-information-to) |
| SIP 404, 410 or 484 | Number not in service, changed or incomplete; check for stale pre-2023 numbers | [RFC 3398](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3398.html) |
| HTTP 429, error 10004, or long queue time | Twilio account concurrency or calls-per-second limit; the call never left Twilio | [Twilio error 10004](https://www.twilio.com/docs/api/errors/10004); [Twilio Call resource](https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/api/call-resource) |
| `completed`, but the audio is a busy tone, fast busy or recorded intercept | An in-band signal that Twilio cannot classify; detect it from the audio | [Twilio Support](https://support.twilio.com/hc/en-us/articles/53558413407003-Detecting-When-a-Recipient-Is-Already-on-Another-Call-With-Twilio-Programmable-Voice) |

Recorded audio can tell busy from congestion. The North American busy tone is 480+620 Hz, on 0.5 s and off 0.5 s (60 interruptions a minute). Reorder, or "fast busy," uses the same frequencies at twice the rate ([Precise Tone Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precise_Tone_Plan); [7 CFR 1755.522](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-7/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1755/section-1755.522)). Under the FCC's March 2025 order, which gave providers twelve months to comply, a provider that blocks calls on analytics must signal it with SIP 603+ and stop using plain 603, 607 or 608 for that purpose ([Federal Register 2025-04811](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/03/24/2025-04811/advanced-methods-to-target-and-eliminate-unlawful-robocalls)). An analytics block is therefore distinguishable from a busy line, and it is a cue to reduce load, not to route around it.

Three checks on calls the system already places answer the remaining questions without adding any load. **First, whether the spoken Zulu time advances from call to call** shows the outlet mode: an advancing time means the station is voicing the one-minute observation, while a time stuck at the METAR hour means the tower has set the outlet to the last METAR. **Second, whether `connect_duration` tracks message length** separates the two disconnect hypotheses: if connected time rises when clouds, weather or remarks lengthen the message, the disconnect counts plays; if it stays fixed regardless of content, it is a timer, and audio that starts mid-word confirms the caller is joined to the loop already playing. **Third, when busies cluster** separates faults from contention: busy that is flat across the day, including the middle of the night, points to a fault worth reporting, while busy that bunches around weather events and market-sensitive times is other callers. Probing the line specifically to measure its occupancy would itself add load; the busy share of ordinary calls, read against time of day, is enough.

## Conclusion

The three symptoms are one design seen from three angles: a single line whose only sharing mechanism is the station's own two-play hang-up. That limits what can change. Switching carriers, upgrading the Twilio account, or the FAA's move from copper to IP changes which box generates the busy signal, not how many callers fit. The port count is set inside the station, and the ASOS 2.0 hardware keeps it at one. Each station's phone line is therefore a fixed budget of about 38–40 connected calls an hour. Pilots, relay services and every automated caller share that budget, so each automated connection comes out of the same pool pilots rely on. Busy rates at market-sensitive minutes are best read as a measure of that crowding, not as a technical fault. Two findings sharpen the picture: a caller's own busy count overstates congestion, and hanging up early may not shorten the station's occupancy if the ASOS ignores the disconnect signal.

The remaining unknowns are the ring count, whether the repeat rule is a count or a timer, how the station handles a caller hanging up first, and what the IP migration did to each line. Only non-public NWS manuals or the ASOS Program Office (suad.asos.pmo@noaa.gov) can settle them. The same office is planning the public high-frequency "proxy" for ASOS data ([NWS ASOS FAQ](https://www.weather.gov/asos/FAQ.html)), which would make the phone line irrelevant for machine use. Until it arrives, the voice line remains an intermittent, contested signal whose limits are structural. Machine feeds and a public one-minute service, not the phone, are the path that can scale.
