# Sub-30-second ASOS data has no continuous legitimate source

No legitimate channel today delivers ASOS one-minute observations (OMO) continuously within ~30 seconds for KNYC, KLAX, KMIA, KPHL, KMDW, KDEN or KAUS. **The only public outlet that speaks the OMO within tens of seconds is the station's own voice line.** It is a single dial-in number per site, sponsored by the FAA for the general-aviation public and designed to hang up after the message plays twice so the next pilot can get through. Holding that line continuously, defeating its auto-disconnect, occupying it with parallel calls, or dialing the password-protected remote-user modem port without NWS authorization are **not legitimate options**: the first three monopolize a shared aviation-safety line, and the last bypasses federal access controls. Used politely (one call at a time per station, let it disconnect, back off on busy, cap volume), the phone gives intermittent reads roughly 30–90 seconds after the minute. That is fast enough to sometimes beat every machine feed, but it cannot give continuous coverage. Every machine-readable one-minute feed (Kalshi's free `live_data` index, Synoptic `1M` push, Wethr.net, minuteTemp, MADIS) traces to one FAA→MADIS→Synoptic batch that lands at **M+2:15 to M+2:40**, and none of them carries KNYC at all. The one official path that might beat that batch is the FAA's CSS-Wx "One Minute Observations" dataset on SWIM, but its latency is undocumented and it reaches outsiders only through a restricted gateway, with a public-cloud release projected for Q4 2026. Every channel, phone included, reports temperature in **whole °C**. The practical plan is: take the free Kalshi batch now, buy Synoptic push for KDEN/KAUS, use the phone as a rate-limited trigger, and ask the FAA and the NWS ASOS program for the public low-latency OMO service both agencies have already put on their roadmaps.

## The fastest KLAX traders moved 21 and 53 seconds after the sensor minute

The case for sub-minute data comes from two KLAX daily-high markets. On **Aug 29, 2026** the KLAX one-minute record shows 31 °C (87.8 °F) for exactly one minute, 19:31Z, with 30 °C either side. The DSM and CLI later reported "MAXIMUM 87 at 11:31 AM" LST, which is 19:31Z. **The first aggressive sweep hit the "87° or above" contract at 19:31:21.166Z**, lifting YES from 0.55 to 0.75 across about 80 price levels while "85° to 86°" was sold 7 ms later. By 19:32:15 the market was settled in all but name. The one-minute machine feed did not deliver that reading until about 19:33:15–19:34:00. The 5-minute HF-METAR samples at 19:30 and 19:35 both read 30 °C, so the 31 °C minute never appeared there, and the 19:53 METAR read 85 °F. On **Aug 30** KLAX first showed 29 °C (84.2 °F) at 18:12Z, and the first sweep of "84° or above" came at **18:12:53.531Z**, about 80 seconds before the machine feed delivered the minute. These timings come from Mercury's own cross-reference of Kalshi trade prints, the Kalshi weather-index API with per-station receipt times, and DSM/CLI issuance times from the IEM AFOS archive ([Kalshi Get Weather Index](https://docs.kalshi.com/api-reference/live-data/get-weather-index); [IEM](https://mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/ASOS)).

**Scheduled-text bots cannot explain these sweeps.** Wethr.net's guide to market bots lists KLAX Daily Summary Messages at about 22:08, 01:08 and 08:08Z ([Wethr market bots](https://wethr.net/edu/market-bots)), and the KLAX DSMs on those days were issued at **22:07Z, 01:07Z and 08:07Z**. None of those is near 19:31:21Z or 18:12:53Z, which **rules out DSM bots for the KLAX case.** This differs from the two widely shared "whale" sweeps that PredictCast documented at KNYC (20:16:15Z on Jul 13) and KDEN (23:17:24Z on Aug 7) ([PredictCast KNYC](https://wxmap.io/predictcast/blog/KNYC-416-whale-trade); [PredictCast KDEN](https://wxmap.io/predictcast/blog/KDEN-517-flash-crash)). Those land on or just after the listed KNYC (~20:14Z) and KDEN (23:17Z) DSM times, so they fit DSM bots better than any exotic channel ([Wethr NYC guide](https://wethr.net/edu/market/newyork)).

What the fast KLAX traders likely used is the ASOS voice line. No METAR, SPECI or DSM was issued near either minute, and the 5-minute stream never showed the Aug 29 value. The lag varied (21 s one day, 53 s the next), which fits listening to a looping voice message better than a fixed-latency data feed. The trader community says the same thing openly: Wethr.net describes an "OMO Bot" that automates calls to the dial-in system, notes that "often, only one call can access the ASOS line at a time," and warns traders about "cities where OMO bots are known to be active" ([Wethr market bots](https://wethr.net/edu/market-bots)). An open-source Polymarket dashboard ships an opt-in calling agent that dials ASOS lines via a VoIP number and transcribes the temperature, with a guardrail to "keep volume to a handful of short calls per day per city" because "these lines exist for pilots" ([testedmedia/polymarket-weather-command-center](https://github.com/testedmedia/polymarket-weather-command-center)). Two caveats apply. First, 19:31:21 is about 2 s earlier than the ASOS guide's nominal OMO release at M+23 s, so either the voice outlet updates slightly sooner or the station clock is offset. Second, a non-public FAA data path cannot be excluded from trade data alone. No public post documents any trader's call cadence, so it cannot be determined whether those callers were well-behaved.

## The pilot phone line is fast but built to be shared

The ASOS voice message is "made available to the general aviation public through FAA sponsored telephone dial-in access," is identical to the ground-to-air radio message, is spoken at 100 words per minute, and carries **"Either OMO or last transmitted METAR/SPECI – Not both"** ([ASOS User's Guide](https://www.weather.gov/media/asos/aum-toc.pdf); [ASOS data-outlet charts](https://www.weather.gov/media/asos/aum-charts2.pdf)). The guide describes "a dial-in telephone number provided at each ASOS location", and no public document gives the number of simultaneous callers. Pilot reports and a commercial relay patent both treat busy signals as normal ([US Patent 7,088,241](https://www.freepatentsonline.com/7088241.html)). The FAA's design standard for non-federal AWOS states the intent plainly: the voice subsystem "should automatically disconnect when the weather observation has been completely transmitted twice," and the minimum requirement is that "the system answers a single call" ([FAA AC 150/5220-16E](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_150_5220-16E.pdf)). The ~90 s two-loop disconnect observed on these lines matches that intent, so it should be treated as a feature of the line rather than an obstacle.

**This is why "a phone system that keeps the bot on the line" is not a legitimate design.** Holding one station's single public line continuously, re-dialing to defeat the disconnect, or running parallel lines against the same number would deny the service to the pilots it exists for. The separate ASOS remote-user port is a different matter: it is a modem interface restricted to "authorized remote users (with modem-equipped computers and the proper access code/password)," used by NWS technicians, AOMC and NCEI's archive collection ([ASOS User's Guide](https://www.weather.gov/media/asos/aum-toc.pdf); [NWS ASOS OID training](https://www.weather.gov/media/surface/ASOS_OID_final111612.pdf)). ASOS software shows a security warning to remote callers and logs silent dial-ins as attempts "to deny service" ([ASOS v3.10 release notes](https://www.weather.gov/media/asos/ASOS%20Implementation/release_notes_310_final.pdf)). Dialing it without NWS authorization is off-limits. Kalshi's own CFTC filing bars "NWS/FAA personnel and contractors responsible for operating, maintaining, calibrating, or repairing" index stations from trading ([CFTC filing ptc09092624308](https://www.cftc.gov/filings/ptc/ptc09092624308.pdf)), which indicates the exchange treats privileged station access as an insider-information problem, not just a technical one. That is a reading of the filing, not a legal conclusion.

The best well-behaved phone approach follows directly from those constraints. Place **at most one call at a time per station**, let the ASOS hang up or hang up yourself as soon as the new minute is heard, back off with jitter on a busy signal instead of redialing, cap calls per station per day, and avoid the top-of-hour period when pilots call most. Transcribe the spoken Zulu time along with the temperature, and validate each reading against the previous minute before acting on it. When connected, a new value should arrive roughly **30–90 s after the minute** (an estimate consistent with the KLAX first movers, not a measured service level). The value is whole °C, which proves only a floor in °F (31 °C → 87 or 88 °F), the same precision as every machine OMO feed. Two station-level checks matter. All six airports are towered, and FAA JO 7900.5E lets a staffed facility switch the voice outlets to the last METAR/SPECI instead of the OMO, while unstaffed sites such as KNYC must broadcast OMO ([FAA JO 7900.5E](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Order/JO_7900.5E_with_Change_1.pdf)). If the spoken time sticks at the METAR time instead of advancing every minute, the call adds nothing over a METAR feed. Telephony cost is trivial: the open-source calling agent budgets **$5–20 per month** ([testedmedia](https://github.com/testedmedia/polymarket-weather-command-center)).

**Pilot apps and datalinks do not offer a back door to the same data.** ForeFlight states it provides only METAR/SPECI and not "the 1-minute weather you'd get over the phone or on the radio broadcast" ([ForeFlight blog](https://blog.foreflight.com/2016/11/10/aging-surface-observations/)). Its 2026 transcribed-ATIS feature, like ATIS Relay's roughly 80-second dial-and-transcribe service, covers ATIS, which updates only with each hourly or special observation ([ForeFlight 18.1](https://www.foreflight.com/releases/18-1); [ATIS Relay](https://www.atisrelay.com/)). anyAWOS ($5.95/month) bridges callers to the same single station line and adds no capacity ([anyAWOS](https://www.anyawos.com/Subscribe.html)). FIS-B's "1 Minute AWOS" product was dropped by the contractor in 2016 ([RTCA SC-206 summary](https://www.rtca.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/sc-206_dec_2016_summary.pdf)), and SiriusXM and Garmin Connext carry METARs on 5–12 minute cycles ([Garmin G1000 NXi guide](https://www.dbq.edu/media/Academics/VPAcademicAffairs/AcademicDepartments/Aviation/G1000-NXi-Pilot's-Guide-Sections-6---7.pdf)). A VHF receiver does not help at these seven sites either. KNYC is not an airport. KDEN, KMDW, KAUS and KLAX publish the ASOS as phone-only, and at KPHL and KMIA the ASOS frequency is shared with ATIS, which the FAA forbids from broadcasting simultaneously with ASOS ([AirNav KPHL](https://www.airnav.com/airport/KPHL); [FAA JO 7900.5E](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Order/JO_7900.5E_with_Change_1.pdf)). LiveATC's terms bar commercial and automated use without a contract ([LiveATC terms](https://www.liveatc.net/legal/)). Seconds-fresh vendor web pages such as Mesotech's AWOS Live exist only for non-federal AWOS airports, not these ASOS stations ([Mesotech AWOS Live](https://mesotech.com/pages/awos-live)).

## Every machine feed shares one batch that lands at M+2:15

The ASOS releases each OMO locally at **M+23 s**, but OMO "is generally not transmitted long-line beyond the local FAA or NWS communications network node" ([ASOS User's Guide](https://www.weather.gov/media/asos/aum-toc.pdf)). The FAA path to the outside world runs ASOS → ADAS → WMSCR → NOAA MADIS, which publishes the dataset with "No restrictions. All observations are publicly accessible" but processes "the current and previous hour's data … every 5 minutes" and excludes LDM ([MADIS 1-minute ASOS](https://madis.ncep.noaa.gov/madis_OMO.shtml)). In a sample of MADIS files on 2026-10-09, records reached MADIS a median **134–139 s** after the observation time, tightly clustered, which points to a scheduled relay rather than network jitter ([MADIS hfmetar directory](https://madis-data.ncep.noaa.gov/madisPublic1/data/LDAD/hfmetar/netCDF/)). The public HTTP files contain only 5-minute samples, and Mercury's polling of them measured 9–20 minutes from observation to availability.

Synoptic Data resells the full one-minute stream as stations with a `1M` suffix, quotes latency "usually … between 2 and 5 minutes," says the source "was unavailable … between October 2023 and January 2026," and still labels it experimental ([Synoptic HF-ASOS](https://docs.synopticdata.com/services/high-frequency-asos)). Its own processing adds under 10 s, and Push Streaming delivers "within 2 seconds" but only for "higher tier commercial accounts" on a quoted price ([Synoptic latency](https://docs.synopticdata.com/services/latency-of-observation-data-on-the-synoptic-platfo); [Synoptic Push Streaming](https://docs.synopticdata.com/services/push-streaming)). Kalshi's index is built on that same Synoptic stream, with a **300-second receipt deadline** written into its CFTC filing ([CFTC filing ptc09092624308](https://www.cftc.gov/filings/ptc/ptc09092624308.pdf)), and Kalshi republishes every member station's reading free at `live_data/weather/{city}?detailed=true` ([Kalshi Get Weather Index](https://docs.kalshi.com/api-reference/live-data/get-weather-index)). Mercury's measurements on 2026-10-09 put Kalshi's receipt at **M+2:16 to M+2:36**, with every `1M` station in every city arriving inside the same ~3-second window and appearing on the free API within ~1–2 s. Wethr.net's independent measurements agree: one-minute median **2m25s**, p90 2m59s across 93 stations ([Wethr latency](https://wethr.net/latency)). minuteTemp's "2–3 seconds" claim is measured from when "Kalshi receives the station batch," not from the observation ([minuteTemp](https://minutetemp.com/)). Enterprise vendors (DTN, AccuWeather, Xweather, Baron, Tomorrow.io) advertise no ASOS one-minute product at all.

The consequence is blunt: **paying more for a different vendor buys convenience, not speed.** Every product on this chain inherits the same ~2-minute FAA→MADIS delay and the same outage risk. KNYC is absent from the MADIS stream and from Wethr's one-minute list, and Kalshi's NYC index uses eight airport stations instead of Central Park. **No continuous machine feed of KNYC one-minute data exists at any price.**

## Only the FAA's CSS-Wx feed could plausibly close the gap

The FAA Data Agreement Portal lists a CSS-Wx dataset called **"Surface Weather Observations - One Minute Observations (OMO) - Surface observations from AWOS/ASOS that are generated every minute,"** delivered over JMS in IWXXM/FAAWX XML ([FAA Agreement Portal](https://aa.data.faa.gov/data/service.jsf?uuid=02422d2b-690f-42ba-9ce3-7a72f830f01c)). The FAA's own answer to the NTSB confirms that OMOs flow from ADAS and WMSCR into CSS-Wx, are translated to IWXXM, and keep "ambient and dewpoint temperature in degrees Celsius"; the sample message reads `airTemperature uom="Cel">11.0` ([NTSB DCA25MA108 Attachment 1](https://data.ntsb.gov/Docket/Document/docBLOB?FileExtension=pdf&FileName=WX_DCA25MA108_Attachment_1_redacted-Rel.pdf&ID=18843279)). It sits upstream of, or parallel to, the hop that adds most of the two minutes. Whether it is actually faster is undocumented: for CSS-Wx to hit a 30 s target, the ADAS→WMSCR→CSS-Wx→gateway chain would have to add under ~7 s beyond the M+23 s release. **It is the only official machine path that could beat the batch, and it must be measured before anyone relies on it.**

Access is the obstacle. Today CSS-Wx reaches outsiders only through the NAS Enterprise Security Gateway, where "NESG SWIM connections are reserved for industry partners that require operational data usage (e.g., airlines, vendors, etc.)." Onboarding means contacting Data-To-Industry@faa.gov, signing agreements, building a VPN to the Atlantic City or Oklahoma City gateway, and passing qualification testing, with "OPS – 3-4 months as a consumer" ([FPAW SWIM briefing](https://fpaw.aero/sites/default/files/197/1a5-kratky-fpaw-swim.pdf)). The data itself is free, and costs fall on the consumer's interface ([FAA SWIM Q&A](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/technology/swim/questions_answers)). The free, self-service SWIM Cloud Distribution Service does not carry CSS-Wx yet. A SWIFT Portal notice dated 07/22/26 says CSS-Wx "is expected to join the SCDS family … in Q4 2026" after the FAA "refined its projections," and does not say whether OMO will be included ([SWIFT Portal](https://portal.swim.faa.gov/)). Because the OMO dataset carries the same standard acknowledgment as METAR, it appears not to be classed as sensitive, so the gate is eligibility and backlog rather than an FAA sponsor contract. KNYC probably will not appear, since it is missing from the WMSCR-fed MADIS stream.

The NWS side offers future relief but nothing exclusive. The ASOS FAQ answers "Can I connect directly to an ASOS?" with "Currently this is not possible, however there are plans to make high-frequency ASOS data available through a proxy" ([NWS ASOS FAQ](https://www.weather.gov/asos/FAQ.html)). The program's ASOS 2.0 plan lists "IP communications – Ubiquitous access to OMO (goal)" and a target to "replace all copper voice and data lines with Internet Protocol communications" by end of 2026 ([FPAW, Boutin](https://fpaw.aero/sites/default/files/163/2-boutin-decadal-look-asos-lifecycle-upgrades.pdf)). Campbell Scientific says the agencies were still in final acceptance testing as of 2026 ([Campbell Scientific](https://www.campbellsci.com/resources/case-studies/us-asos-weather-network)), and the NWS equipment sheet shows all seven stations on legacy processors with an empty "ASOS 2.0 In Service Dates" tab ([NWS ASOS equipment](https://www.weather.gov/asos/asosequip.html)). KMDW and KAUS are FAA-owned and exposed to the FAA's decision to move 207 ASOS sites to AWOS-C between 2025 and 2029, which could change their voice lines and one-minute outputs ([NWS ASOS FAQ](https://www.weather.gov/asos/FAQ.html)). **A privileged private feed is not on offer**: NWS policy says it "will not provide an environmental information service to one entity unless it can also be provided to other similar entities" ([NWSPD 1-10](https://www.weather.gov/media/directives/001_pdfs/pd00110curr.pdf)). The productive request is for the public proxy, on a published schedule, for everyone. The ASOS guide's on-airport option, user-provided "non-interactive video monitors" (up to 50 per airport) showing the same data as the VDU, refreshed each minute, is the one official near-real-time hookup ([ASOS User's Guide](https://www.weather.gov/media/asos/aum-toc.pdf)). It needs physical presence at each airport and agency approval for a non-aviation user, so it is a long shot.

## Conclusion

The ~2-minute gap is a property of the FAA→NOAA relay, not of any vendor, so it cannot be bought away. It can only be bypassed by hearing the station directly or by tapping the FAA chain earlier. The first route is open to everyone, which is why it is contested and why the honest ceiling for a well-behaved caller is intermittent leads, not a continuous feed. The second route depends on the FAA, and the best evidence is that the agencies already intend to open it publicly, through CSS-Wx on SCDS and the NWS "proxy." The edge from the phone is therefore temporary: once a public OMO feed lands, the voice line stops being the fastest path, and the edge shifts to whoever has measured and integrated that feed first. For KNYC, where no machine stream exists, the phone stays the only minute path until NWS adds Central Park to whatever replaces it. Because every channel is whole °C, a faster OMO tells you the floor of the day's high, never its exact °F value. Speed decides the bucket edge only when the floor alone kills the bucket below it, as it did on both KLAX days.
